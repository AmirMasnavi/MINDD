\chapter*{Abstract}
In this first task we studied 441,077 electric-vehicle charging sessions. The goal of the project is to predict,
when a session starts, whether it will end abnormally. The file has no target column, so we built it from the
end cause: the 13 causes that are faults count as abnormal, which gives 18.58\% abnormal sessions. We checked
this definition against the dataset's own history columns, and they match in about 100\% of the rows.

Several columns are only known after the session ends (energy, costs, end and payment times, end cause), so
we removed them from the predictors, together with the order creation time. We found no duplicates or invalid
records. The missing values have a clear reason and the extreme values are real, so we did not remove any
row. Volume, abnormal share and type of fault change over the two years, so we split the data by time and
not at random.

We keep 29 features in 7 groups. The recent failure history of the charging post was the strongest signal we
found.

%-------------------------------------------------------------- 1
\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

The project asks whether the information available when an \ac{EV} charging session starts can predict that the
session will end abnormally. We work with an educational version of the China EV Charging Dataset
\cite{zhang2025}: 441,077 sessions and 47 columns, from 31 Dec 2019 to 31 Dec 2021, at 92 charging posts in 13
stations and 3 districts. All the numbers in this report come from our notebook
\nolinkurl{Task1.ipynb} (seed 42).

We followed three rules during the whole task. First, a variable can only be a predictor if it is known when the
session starts. Second, before fixing a problem in the data, we tried to understand it. Third, any value learned
from the data (for example, the mean used for scaling) is learned from the training period only.

\section{Target definition}\label{sec:target}

The brief describes the target \col{is_Abnormal}, but the file does not include it. It only has \col{end_cause},
with 15 values. We decided that a session is \emph{normal} when it ends with ``Charging ends normally'' or ``User
stops charging'', because stopping is the user's own choice. The other 13 causes are equipment, communication or
vehicle faults, so we label them \emph{abnormal}.

To check this choice, we used the history columns of the dataset. For each session, they count how many earlier
sessions of the same post or user were abnormal. We recounted them with our label and with an alternative where
only ``Charging ends normally'' is normal. Our definition reproduces the supplied counts almost exactly, and the
alternative does not (Table~\ref{tab:target}). This means the dataset was built with the same definition as ours.

\begin{table}[!htb]
  \centering\small
  \caption{Candidate target definitions and agreement with the supplied history columns}
  \label{tab:target}
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{Lrrr}
    \toprule
    Definition of ``normal'' & Post, 30 days & User, 30 days & Abnormal share \\
    \midrule
    A: ends normally + user stops & 100.00\% & 99.98\% & 18.58\% \\
    B: ends normally only & 0.26\% & 35.85\% & 53.93\% \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

With this definition there is about 1 abnormal session for every 4.4 normal ones. A model that always predicts
``normal'' would already be 81\% accurate, so accuracy alone is not a good metric here. We also noticed two
details. The supplied rates are smoothed as (abnormal\,+\,1)\,/\,(sessions\,+\,5), so a post or user with no
history always gets the rate 0.2. In addition, 9,058 normal sessions (2.5\%) delivered no energy, mostly users
who stopped after about one minute. We kept them as normal, because removing them would require the energy, which
is only known after the session.

\chapter{Data Audit and Exploration}\label{ch:audit}

\section{Availability at prediction time}\label{sec:leakage}

Seven columns describe what happened during the session: energy, electricity cost, service charge, amount,
actual payment, end time and payment time. \col{end_cause} is the outcome itself. Figure~\ref{fig:auc} shows why
these columns are a problem. On its own, the energy, each money column and the duration separate normal and
abnormal sessions with an \ac{ROC-AUC} of 0.82--0.85, better than any variable known at the start (best 0.77). The
payment delay is weaker (0.61), but it is also only known after the session. The reason is that abnormal sessions
are short and deliver little energy, so these columns describe the result instead of predicting it, so all of them
were excluded.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.56\textwidth]{08_1_univariate_auc_by_availability.png}
  \caption[Univariate ROC-AUC on the training period, coloured by availability at the start]{Univariate \acs{ROC-AUC} on the training period, coloured by availability at the start}
  \label{fig:auc}
\end{figure}

The order creation time was a harder case. It is stamped after the start in 56\% of the sessions (57.2\% in the
training period, sometimes 16\,h later), so we cannot be sure it exists when we make the prediction. Its link to
the outcome is also weak (\acs{AUC} 0.59), so we excluded it too. Weather has only one value per district and day.
Following the brief, we treat it as a forecast that is known at the start.

\section{Data quality}\label{sec:quality}

Table~\ref{tab:quality} lists the checks we made. In general, the records are in good condition. The missing
values appear only when there was no earlier session or failure, so they are not really unknown. For the
outliers, we first used the \ac{IQR} rule and then looked at the extreme cases one by one. They turned out to be real
situations, so we did not remove any row.

\section{What relates to abnormal termination}\label{sec:relations}

We measured these relationships on the training period only, so that the validation and test periods do not
influence our choices. Location matters, but mostly at the level of the post. The abnormal share is 14.6--24.1\% per district and about
8--38\% per station, while the posts range from 4.1\% to 60.9\%. The 10 worst posts produce 24.1\% of the
abnormal sessions with only 10.2\% of all sessions. Calendar and weather effects are small. Across the hours of
the day the abnormal share moves between 14.7\% and 20.5\%, across the days of the week between 17.0\% and
18.9\%, and the weather effects are weak and not linear.

The history columns gave the clearest signal (Figure~\ref{fig:history}). When we split the 7-day post rate into
deciles, the abnormal share goes from about 4\% in the lowest decile to about 60\% in the highest. In other
words, a post that failed recently tends to fail again. User history also helps, but less: its \acs{AUC} is around
0.69--0.70, against 0.75--0.77 for post history.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{09_4_history_deciles.png}
  \caption{Abnormal share by decile of six history variables (dashed line: training average)}
  \label{fig:history}
\end{figure}

The users are very unequal. In the training period, 45\% of the 34,294 accounts have only one session, but the
top 1\% of accounts have 34\% of the sessions. New users (12\% of the sessions) fail a little more often than
returning users (20.2\% against 17.5\%).

\section{Changes over time}\label{sec:temporal}

Figure~\ref{fig:monthly} shows that the data are not stable over the two years. Monthly volume drops to 1,391
sessions in February 2020, probably because of COVID-19, and reaches 25--28k sessions per month in autumn 2021.
The abnormal share falls from 26.3\% to 13.7\% and later rises again to 22.9\%. The type of fault also changes:
connection faults add 7.1 percentage points to the abnormal share in Q1-2020, but only 1--2 points from Q3-2020
onwards. At the start of the file the history columns are still incomplete, which is why 31\% of the sessions in
January 2020 come from ``new'' users, against about 9--15\% later. Finally, posts 17--20 stop on 28 Sep 2021.
Some things are stable, though. The abnormal share of each post is similar in 2020 and 2021 (Spearman 0.81),
which explains why post history works. Because of all the changes above, a random split would mix different
periods and give results that are too optimistic, so we split the data by time.

\chapter{Preparation}\label{ch:prep}

\section{Chronological split}\label{sec:split}

We did not use the sessions before 1 April 2020 (the \emph{burn-in}), because their history columns are
incomplete and their fault mix no longer exists. The remaining data were split by start date into training,
validation and test periods (Table~\ref{tab:split}). The label of a session is only known when it ends, so we
also removed (\emph{purged}) the 28 training sessions that end after the training cut-off.

\begin{table}[!htb]
  \centering\small
  \caption{Chronological split}
  \label{tab:split}
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.2cm}Lrr}
    \toprule
    Period & Dates & Sessions & Abnormal \\
    \midrule
    Burn-in (not used) & 31 Dec 2019 to 31 Mar 2020 & 14,017 & 25.0\% \\
    Train & 1 Apr 2020 to 30 Jun 2021 & 271,182 & 17.8\% \\
    Purged & Train starts that end after 1 Jul 2021 & 28 & -- \\
    Validation & Jul to Sep 2021 & 75,769 & 21.1\% \\
    Test (used once) & Oct to Dec 2021 & 80,081 & 17.6\% \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The split shows two things that matter for Task 2. First, 39.9\% of the validation sessions and 61.3\% of the
test sessions come from accounts that never appear in training, so \col{user_id} cannot be a feature. Second,
every post in validation and test was already seen in training, so we cannot test a brand-new post. For tuning,
we will use expanding-window folds inside the training period instead of a random K-fold.

\section{Features}\label{sec:features}

We keep 29 features in 7 groups: calendar (hour and day of week), tariff period, location (\col{post_id},
station and district), weather (temperature, humidity, precipitation and a new \col{rain_flag}), post history
(8), user history (10) and concurrency (1). Table~\ref{tab:dropped} lists the start-time columns we dropped.

\begin{table}[!htb]
  \centering\small
  \caption{Start-time columns that were dropped}
  \label{tab:dropped}
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{7.2cm}L}
    \toprule
    Column & Reason \\
    \midrule
    \col{electricity_price} & Fixed by the tariff period \\
    Six abnormal-count columns & Exact functions of the session counts and rates \\
    Two post ``no history'' flags & Always 0 after the burn-in \\
    \col{user_no_previous_completed_session} & Same as \col{is_new_user} in 99.9\% of rows \\
    \col{user_id} & Key field with 56k values; most test accounts are new \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

Some features are strongly related to each other. Only the user-history windows have a \ac{VIF} above 10 (up to
65.4), because the 180-day and 730-day windows contain almost the same sessions. Tree models and regularised
linear models can deal with this, so they get all the features. For plain linear or distance-based models we
built a smaller set. We went through the features from the best to the worst univariate \acs{AUC} and kept a feature
only if its $|\rho|$ with the features already kept was below 0.90. This leaves 19 of the 23 numeric features and
brings the maximum \acs{VIF} down to 8.6.

\ac{PCA} was also tested. It needs 11 of the 23 components to keep 90\% of the variance, and the first component hardly
separates the classes (\acs{AUC} 0.51). Since it compresses little and would mix post and user information, we did not
use it. Discretisation was only used to explore the data (deciles and weather bands), not as a model input.

\section{Learned operations and class imbalance}\label{sec:prep}

Different models need different preparation, so we prepared two versions of the data. The tree version keeps the
raw values, the \acs{NaN} and the category codes. The linear version applies \texttt{log1p} to 8 skewed columns, fills
the structural \acs{NaN} with the training maximum (we read ``never happened'' as ``a very long time ago'') and keeps
the flags. It then standardises the numeric columns, encodes hour and weekday with sine and cosine, and one-hot
encodes \col{post_id} and the tariff period. The result has 126 columns and no \acs{NaN}.

All operations that learn something are fitted on the training period only, inside a scikit-learn pipeline
(Table~\ref{tab:ledger}). We checked what would happen if we fitted them on all periods instead, and the learned
values change. For example, the fill value for ``days since the user's last failure'' would move from 543 to 702
days, which would leak information from the validation and test periods.

\begin{table}[!htb]
  \centering\small
  \caption{Learned operations, all fitted on the training period}
  \label{tab:ledger}
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{LLL}
    \toprule
    Operation & What is learned & Applies to \\
    \midrule
    One-hot encoding / category codes & List of categories & Linear / tree version \\
    Structural-\acs{NaN} fill & Training maximum (flags kept) & Linear version \\
    Standardisation & Mean and standard deviation & Linear version \\
    Log-transform rule & Which columns are skewed & Linear version (8 columns) \\
    Reduced feature set & Spearman $\rho$ and \acs{AUC} (training sample) & Plain linear / distance models \\
    Class-imbalance treatment & Class weights or resampling & Training folds only (Task 2) \\
    Outlier capping, \acs{PCA}, rare-category grouping & Nothing & Not used / not needed \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

The classes (about 1\,:\,4.4) are not balanced in the prepared data. If Task 2 uses class weights or
resampling, they will be applied only inside the training folds, so validation and test keep their real
distribution. We will be careful with \ac{SMOTE}, because interpolating between sessions can create values that
cannot exist, such as half a flag or a mix of two posts.

\chapter{Synthesis}\label{ch:synthesis}

The most important thing we learned is that the outcome is easy to ``predict'' with the wrong columns. Energy,
cost and duration separate the classes better than anything known at the start, but only because they describe
the result. Once these columns are removed, the best signal is the recent failure history of each post. Location
and user history add some information, while calendar and weather add little. Our second main finding is that
the data change over time, in volume, in abnormal share and in the type of fault. The records were in good
condition, so no row was removed or imputed, and we keep 29 features in 7 groups (19 numeric features for plain
linear models).

Some questions remain open. We do not know whether the daily weather values were a forecast or were measured
during the day; in the second case they would contain information from after the start. Durations stop at
24\,h, and we could not explain the timing of the order and payment fields. Our explanations for the changes over
time, such as COVID-19, are hypotheses. We also cannot test a new post, and user history is a behaviour profile,
which raises a privacy question.

For Task 2, we will evaluate the models on the time split, use the test period only once and report precision,
recall and \ac{PR-AUC} against the base rate of each period. We plan to start with tree ensembles and a regularised
logistic regression, and to run the feature-group ablation with and without \col{post_id} and user history.
