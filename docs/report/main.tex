\chapter*{Abstract}
In this first task we studied 441,077 electric-vehicle charging sessions. The goal of the project is to predict,
when a session starts, whether it will end abnormally. The file has no target column, so we built it from the
end cause: the 13 causes that are faults count as abnormal, which gives 18.58\% abnormal sessions. We checked
this definition against the dataset's own history columns, and they match in about 100\% of the rows.

Several columns are only known after the session ends (energy, costs, end and payment times, end cause), so
we removed them from the predictors, together with the order creation time. We found no duplicates or invalid
records. The missing values have a clear reason and the extreme values are real, so we did not remove any
row. Volume, abnormal share and type of fault change over the two years, so we split the data by time and
not at random.

We keep 29 features in 7 groups. The recent failure history of the charging post was the strongest signal we
found.

In the second task we fixed how a solution will be evaluated, before training any model. A prediction is a risk
alert raised when the session starts. The abnormal class is the minority and a missed failure costs more than a
false alert, so the primary metric is the area under the precision--recall curve (PR-AUC), always read together
with the abnormal share of the period. Models will be compared on four expanding-window folds, one per quarter,
and the last quarter of 2021 stays untouched as the final test. Two baselines give the reference: a no-skill
baseline, and the 7-day failure rate of the post, which already reaches a PR-AUC of 0.51 against 0.18.

%-------------------------------------------------------------- 1
\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:intro}

The project asks whether the information available when an \ac{EV} charging session starts can predict that the
session will end abnormally. We work with an educational version of the China EV Charging Dataset
\cite{zhang2025}: 441,077 sessions and 47 columns, from 31 Dec 2019 to 31 Dec 2021, at 92 charging posts in 13
stations and 3 districts. All the numbers in this report come from our notebooks
\nolinkurl{Task1.ipynb} and \nolinkurl{Task2.ipynb} (seed 42).

We followed three rules during the whole project. First, a variable can only be a predictor if it is known when the
session starts. Second, before fixing a problem in the data, we tried to understand it. Third, any value learned
from the data (for example, the mean used for scaling) is learned from the training period only.

\section{Target definition}\label{sec:target}

The brief describes the target \col{is_Abnormal}, but the file does not include it. It only has \col{end_cause},
with 15 values. We decided that a session is \emph{normal} when it ends with ``Charging ends normally'' or ``User
stops charging'', because stopping is the user's own choice. The other 13 causes are equipment, communication or
vehicle faults, so we label them \emph{abnormal}.

To check this choice, we used the history columns of the dataset. For each session, they count how many earlier
sessions of the same post or user were abnormal. We recounted them with our label and with an alternative where
only ``Charging ends normally'' is normal. Our definition reproduces the supplied counts almost exactly, and the
alternative does not (Table~\ref{tab:target}). This means the dataset was built with the same definition as ours.

\begin{table}[!htb]
  \centering\small
  \caption{Candidate target definitions and agreement with the supplied history columns}
  \label{tab:target}
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{Lrrr}
    \toprule
    Definition of ``normal'' & Post, 30 days & User, 30 days & Abnormal share \\
    \midrule
    A: ends normally + user stops & 100.00\% & 99.98\% & 18.58\% \\
    B: ends normally only & 0.26\% & 35.85\% & 53.93\% \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

With this definition there is about 1 abnormal session for every 4.4 normal ones. A model that always predicts
``normal'' would already be 81\% accurate, so accuracy alone is not a good metric here. We also noticed two
details. The supplied rates are smoothed as (abnormal\,+\,1)\,/\,(sessions\,+\,5), so a post or user with no
history always gets the rate 0.2. In addition, 9,058 normal sessions (2.5\%) delivered no energy, mostly users
who stopped after about one minute. We kept them as normal, because removing them would require the energy, which
is only known after the session.

\chapter{Data Audit and Exploration}\label{ch:audit}

\section{Availability at prediction time}\label{sec:leakage}

Seven columns describe what happened during the session: energy, electricity cost, service charge, amount,
actual payment, end time and payment time. \col{end_cause} is the outcome itself. Figure~\ref{fig:auc} shows why
these columns are a problem. On its own, the energy, each money column and the duration separate normal and
abnormal sessions with an \ac{ROC-AUC} of 0.82--0.85, better than any variable known at the start (best 0.77). The
payment delay is weaker (0.61), but it is also only known after the session. The reason is that abnormal sessions
are short and deliver little energy, so these columns describe the result instead of predicting it, so all of them
were excluded.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.56\textwidth]{08_1_univariate_auc_by_availability.png}
  \caption[Univariate ROC-AUC on the training period, coloured by availability at the start]{Univariate \acs{ROC-AUC} on the training period, coloured by availability at the start}
  \label{fig:auc}
\end{figure}

The order creation time was a harder case. It is stamped after the start in 56\% of the sessions (57.2\% in the
training period, sometimes 16\,h later), so we cannot be sure it exists when we make the prediction. Its link to
the outcome is also weak (\acs{AUC} 0.59), so we excluded it too. Weather has only one value per district and day.
Following the brief, we treat it as a forecast that is known at the start.

\section{What relates to abnormal termination}\label{sec:relations}

We measured these relationships on the training period only, so that the validation and test periods do not
influence our choices. Location matters, but mostly at the level of the post. The abnormal share is 14.6--24.1\% per district and about
8--38\% per station, while the posts range from 4.1\% to 60.9\%. The 10 worst posts produce 24.1\% of the
abnormal sessions with only 10.2\% of all sessions. Calendar and weather effects are small. Across the hours of
the day the abnormal share moves between 14.7\% and 20.5\%, across the days of the week between 17.0\% and
18.9\%, and the weather effects are weak and not linear.

The history columns gave the clearest signal (Figure~\ref{fig:history}). When we split the 7-day post rate into
deciles, the abnormal share goes from about 4\% in the lowest decile to about 60\% in the highest. In other
words, a post that failed recently tends to fail again. User history also helps, but less: its \acs{AUC} is around
0.69--0.70, against 0.75--0.77 for post history.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{09_4_history_deciles.png}
  \caption{Abnormal share by decile of six history variables (dashed line: training average)}
  \label{fig:history}
\end{figure}

The users are very unequal. In the training period, 45\% of the 34,294 accounts have only one session, but the
top 1\% of accounts have 34\% of the sessions. New users (12\% of the sessions) fail a little more often than
returning users (20.2\% against 17.5\%).

\section{Changes over time}\label{sec:temporal}

Figure~\ref{fig:monthly} shows that the data are not stable over the two years. Monthly volume drops to 1,391
sessions in February 2020, probably because of COVID-19, and reaches 25--28k sessions per month in autumn 2021.
The abnormal share falls from 26.3\% to 13.7\% and later rises again to 22.9\%. The type of fault also changes:
connection faults add 7.1 percentage points to the abnormal share in Q1-2020, but only 1--2 points from Q3-2020
onwards. At the start of the file the history columns are still incomplete, which is why 31\% of the sessions in
January 2020 come from ``new'' users, against about 9--15\% later. Finally, posts 17--20 stop on 28 Sep 2021.
Some things are stable, though. The abnormal share of each post is similar in 2020 and 2021 (Spearman 0.81),
which explains why post history works. Because of all the changes above, a random split would mix different
periods and give results that are too optimistic, so we split the data by time.

\chapter{Preparation}\label{ch:prep}

\section{Chronological split}\label{sec:split}

We did not use the sessions before 1 April 2020 (the \emph{burn-in}), because their history columns are
incomplete and their fault mix no longer exists. The remaining data were split by start date into training,
validation and test periods (Table~\ref{tab:split}). The label of a session is only known when it ends, so we
also removed (\emph{purged}) the 28 training sessions that end after the training cut-off.

The split shows two things that matter for the evaluation. First, 39.9\% of the validation sessions and 61.3\% of the
test sessions come from accounts that never appear in training, so \col{user_id} cannot be a feature. Second,
every post in validation and test was already seen in training, so we cannot test a brand-new post. The training
and validation periods together form the development data. Chapter~\ref{ch:eval} divides them into four
expanding-window folds, and the validation quarter of Table~\ref{tab:split} is the fourth one, so we do not use a
random K-fold.

We keep 29 features in 7 groups: calendar (hour and day of week), tariff period, location (\col{post_id},
station and district), weather (temperature, humidity, precipitation and a new \col{rain_flag}), post history
(8), user history (10) and concurrency (1). Table~\ref{tab:dropped} lists the start-time columns we dropped.

Some features are strongly related to each other. Only the user-history windows have a \ac{VIF} above 10 (up to
65.4), because the 180-day and 730-day windows contain almost the same sessions. Tree models and regularised
linear models can deal with this, so they get all the features. For plain linear or distance-based models we
built a smaller set. We went through the features from the best to the worst univariate \acs{AUC} and kept a feature
only if its $|\rho|$ with the features already kept was below 0.90. This leaves 19 of the 23 numeric features and
brings the maximum \acs{VIF} down to 8.6.

\ac{PCA} was also tested. It needs 11 of the 23 components to keep 90\% of the variance, and the first component hardly
separates the classes (\acs{AUC} 0.51). Since it compresses little and would mix post and user information, we did not
use it. Discretisation was only used to explore the data (deciles and weather bands), not as a model input.

\section{Learned operations and class imbalance}\label{sec:prep}

Different models need different preparation, so we prepared two versions of the data. The tree version keeps the
raw values, the \acs{NaN} and the category codes. The linear version applies \texttt{log1p} to 8 skewed columns, fills
the structural \acs{NaN} with the training maximum (we read ``never happened'' as ``a very long time ago'') and keeps
the flags. It then standardises the numeric columns, encodes hour and weekday with sine and cosine, and one-hot
encodes \col{post_id} and the tariff period. The result has 126 columns and no \acs{NaN}.

All operations that learn something are fitted on the training period only, inside a scikit-learn pipeline
(Table~\ref{tab:ledger}). We checked what would happen if we fitted them on all periods instead, and the learned
values change. For example, the fill value for ``days since the user's last failure'' would move from 543 to 702
days, which would leak information from the validation and test periods.

\begin{table}[!htb]
  \centering\small
  \caption{Learned operations, all fitted on the training period}
  \label{tab:ledger}
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{LLL}
    \toprule
    Operation & What is learned & Applies to \\
    \midrule
    One-hot encoding / category codes & List of categories & Linear / tree version \\

The classes (about 1\,:\,4.4) are not balanced in the prepared data. If the modelling task uses class weights or
resampling, they will be applied only inside the training folds, so validation and test keep their real
distribution. We will be careful with \ac{SMOTE}, because interpolating between sessions can create values that
cannot exist, such as half a flag or a mix of two posts.

%-------------------------------------------------------------- 4
\chapter{Prediction and Evaluation Setting}\label{ch:eval}

In the second task we fixed what exactly is predicted and how a solution will be judged, before training any
model. The notebook \nolinkurl{Task2.ipynb} fits no model. It builds the evaluation protocol and checks it with
two simple baselines.

\section{Prediction problem}\label{sec:problem}

The unit of observation is one charging session, that is, one transaction of one account at one charging post.
The positive class is an abnormal ending (\col{is_Abnormal}\,=\,1, one of the 13 fault causes). The negative
class is a normal ending or a stop requested by the user, as defined in Section~\ref{sec:target}. In the
development period (April 2020 to September 2021), 18.5\% of the 346,979 sessions are abnormal.

The prediction is made once, at the start of the session, with only the information known at that moment. We
designed it for preventive monitoring and maintenance prioritisation. The sessions that are starting, and through
them the posts, are ranked by their risk of failure, so that an operator with limited capacity looks first at the
riskiest ones. Predicting a session as abnormal is therefore a risk alert: this session, on this post, has an
increased risk of ending in a failure. It is not a diagnosis and it does not say which fault will happen. The
positive class also mixes 13 causes whose mix changes over time (Section~\ref{sec:temporal}), so a model could
detect one type of failure well and miss another.

\section{Variables available at the start}\label{sec:variables}

We placed each of the 47 supplied columns in one of the four categories of the brief
(Table~\ref{tab:classification}). The notebook builds this table in code and checks that every column appears
exactly once.

We exclude the seven after-session columns and \col{end_cause} because a model that used them could not run when
the session starts. \col{UserID} is excluded because 35\% to 46\% of the validation sessions of each
fold come from accounts that are not in the training window. A model would only memorise accounts, and it would
identify people. The user history is the legitimate substitute.

The eligible set depends on assumptions that the data cannot prove. Weather is taken as a forecast, as the brief
says. The history columns are taken as supplied. In production they keep updating, so in a validation period they
already contain the outcomes of earlier sessions of that period that had finished. User history needs the platform
to identify the account at the start, otherwise the user group is missing (the cold-start case). Finally,
\col{post_id} is also an identifier, but the post is known at the start and is the most informative location
level, so it stays, and the later ablation will show whether it adds value.

\section{Consequences of the two errors}\label{sec:errors}

A \ac{FP} flags a normal session as risky, and a \ac{FN} lets an abnormal session pass unnoticed. What they cost
depends on the action that follows the alert. With automated monitoring, a false alert is one more line on a
dashboard, while a missed failure means that the next sessions on that post are not watched. With an inspection or
a maintenance visit, a false alert wastes technician time, while a missed failure leaves a faulty post in service.
Failures cluster by post (Figure~\ref{fig:history}), so one missed failure is usually followed by more. Warning the
driver is the most delicate action, because false warnings make drivers stop trusting the alerts, so we left it
out of the main use.

For a single event, we consider the false negative the more expensive error, because the action after an alert is
cheap compared with a failure that continues unseen. However, the number of false alerts is limited by the
capacity of the operators, and a very long list of alerts becomes useless. The application therefore needs a good
ranking, with the real failures near the top, and a fixed number of alerts that the team can handle.

We do not know the cost ratio $c = \text{cost(FN)}/\text{cost(FP)}$. With calibrated probabilities the best
cut-off would be $t^{*} = 1/(1+c)$, which gives 0.50 for $c=1$, 0.33 for $c=2$ and 0.17 for $c=5$. For $c$ above
4.4 the cut-off is below the abnormal share and almost every session would be flagged. Fixing a cut-off now would
be arbitrary. Instead, we chose metrics that judge the ranking and the quality of the probabilities, and we report
the operational results for fixed alert budgets (5\%, 10\% and 20\% of the sessions flagged). The cut-off will be
chosen later, from the cost ratio and the capacity.

\section{Evaluation metrics}\label{sec:metrics}

Table~\ref{tab:metrics} lists the metrics, which we chose before optimising any model.

\begin{table}[!htb]
  \centering\small
  \caption{Evaluation metrics, their role and their limitation in this problem}
  \label{tab:metrics}
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.4cm}LL}
    \toprule
    Metric (role) & Why we use it & Limitation here \\
    \midrule

    \addlinespace[2pt]

    \addlinespace[2pt]

We do not use accuracy. Always predicting ``normal'' is right in 79--84\% of the sessions of each fold and catches
no failure. F1 and similar metrics need a cut-off that is not chosen yet, and F1 gives the same weight to both
errors, so they can only be reported once the final cut-off exists.

\section{Temporal evaluation}\label{sec:folds}

The sessions cannot be divided at random, because volume, abnormal share and fault mix change over time
(Section~\ref{sec:temporal}) and a model is always used on future sessions. Training sessions must come before
the sessions that evaluate them. We keep the burn-in and the test period of Section~\ref{sec:split} and divide the
development period into four expanding-window folds (Table~\ref{tab:folds} and Figure~\ref{fig:folds}). Each fold
trains on the sessions that started and ended before its validation quarter. The fourth fold is exactly the
training and validation periods of Table~\ref{tab:split}.

The boundaries are calendar quarters, fixed before looking at any model result. A quarter is about one season. It
is large enough for a stable estimate (every validation quarter has at least 6,600 abnormal sessions) and short
enough to show changes over time. Four folds are a compromise: an earlier fold would train on less than six months
of data, and monthly folds would give 12 small and noisy estimates. The window is expanding because the model that
would be deployed is trained on all the past.

The folds are not equal. The validation quarters have between 41,797 and 75,769 sessions, and the training window
grows from 6 to 15 months, so the first folds train on less data and do not include a full winter. We expect their
scores to be a little pessimistic. The label of a session is only known when it ends, so the 27 to 39 training
sessions per fold that were still running at the boundary are purged.

Every operation that learns from the data (Table~\ref{tab:ledger}) is fitted again inside each training fold,
together with the model, and applied unchanged to the validation quarter. Class weights or resampling, if used,
change the training rows only. Even a simple statistic differs between folds: the class prior goes from 17.7\% to
18.9\%, and no fold has the 18.5\% of the whole development period. The difference is small here, but it would be
larger for steps that use the target.

To protect the test period, the Task 2 notebook counts its 80,081 sessions and removes them right after reading
the file. Model, hyperparameters and cut-off will be chosen with the four folds only. The
cut-off will be chosen on the out-of-fold predictions of the four folds together and then frozen. The final model
will be refitted on the whole development period and scored once on the test.

\section{Aggregation across folds}\label{sec:aggregation}

We rank the models by the unweighted mean of the \ac{PR-AUC} of the four folds. We want a model that works in a
future period, so each period counts once. Next to it we report the mean weighted by the number of validation
sessions, which reflects the operational volume, and the pooled value over all out-of-fold predictions, which is
needed to choose the cut-off but is dominated by the large folds. Stability is described by the value in each
fold, the standard deviation, the worst fold and the trend. An improving trend is partly expected, because later
folds train on more months, while a falling trend would be a sign of drift.

The abnormal share differs between folds, so the scores of two folds cannot be compared directly, but the
difference between two models on the same fold can. We therefore compare models fold by fold, with the paired
differences and the number of folds won. With four folds, the smallest possible $p$-value of an exact Wilcoxon
test is 0.125, even when a model wins in every fold, so we do not claim statistical significance for these
comparisons. When two models are within about one standard deviation of each other, we will prefer the simpler and
more stable one.

\chapter{Synthesis}\label{ch:synthesis}

The project asks to what extent the information available when a charging session starts can predict that it
ends abnormally. The file has no target column, so we built it from the end cause, and the dataset's own history
columns reproduce our definition almost exactly (Table~\ref{tab:target}). The columns that separate the classes best (energy,
cost and duration, with an \acs{AUC} of 0.82--0.85) describe the result of the session, so they cannot be
predictors. The best variable known at the start reaches 0.77. It is the recent failure history of the post: the
abnormal share goes from about 4\% in the lowest decile of the 7-day rate to about 60\% in the highest. User
history comes second (0.69--0.70), and calendar and weather add little. The records needed no repair. We found no
duplicates or invalid values, the missing values only mean that there was no earlier session or failure, and the
extreme values are real situations, so no row was removed or imputed. We keep 29 features in 7 groups, and 19
numeric ones for plain linear models.

The data change over the two years. The abnormal share moves between 13.7\% and 26.3\%, and connection faults,
which added 7.1 points in the first quarter of 2020, add only 1--2 points from the third quarter on. For this
reason the sessions are split by time, the first three months are left out as a burn-in, and every score is read
against the abnormal share of its own period (Chapter~\ref{ch:eval}). Under this protocol, the 7-day failure rate
of the post, used alone and without fitting anything, reaches a mean \acs{PR-AUC} of 0.511 on the four validation
quarters, against 0.181 for a baseline with no skill. With alerts on 5\% of the sessions, 74\% of the alerts are
real failures, but only 21\% of the failures are caught. So the information available at the start carries a signal,
and a model has to show that it catches more failures than this rule with the same number of alerts.

We treat the weather as a forecast, as the brief says. It has one value per district and day, and if it was
measured during the day it contains information from after the start. Durations stop at 24\,h, and we could not
explain the timing of the order and payment fields. COVID-19 as the reason for the drop in volume in February
2020 is our hypothesis. Every post of the validation and test periods was already seen in training, so we cannot
test a new post, and user history is a behaviour profile of an account, which raises a privacy question. For the
evaluation, the cost ratio between a missed failure and a false alert and the alert capacity are unknown, so the
alert budgets are assumptions and the final cut-off will need one too. The four folds are used for selection, so
their scores are slightly optimistic, and the test gives the unbiased estimate. The 7-day window of baseline B1
was taken from the Task~1 ranking, whose training period overlaps the validation quarters of folds 1 to 3. B1
fits nothing, so we expect this effect to be small. Finally, the target merges 13 fault causes whose mix changes
over time, so a good average could hide a type of failure that the model never detects.

In the modelling task we plan to start with tree ensembles and a regularised logistic regression, compared on the
four folds, and to use the feature-group ablation to check whether \col{post_id} and user history are worth
keeping.
